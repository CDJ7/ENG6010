# Readings 5: Domain Randomization Experiment

## Program Explanation:

This program is a small sim-to-real experiment. It tests whether domain randomization makes a learned walking controller hold up when the physics it meets are different from the physics it trained on. The robot is my own MuJoCo model: a planar two-legged "bounding" robot that works like a 2D slice through a quadruped. It has a torso, a back leg and a front leg, and a hip and a knee on each leg. The process begins by training a walking policy with PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization) from Stable-Baselines3. The reward pays the robot for moving forward up to a target speed of 1.0 m/s, gives a small alive bonus each step, and subtracts small penalties for torque use and for tilting the body. The episode ends as soon as the robot falls. I chose PPO because the Gurram, Uttam & Ohol (2024) survey names it as the most common algorithm in RL quadruped locomotion. I also followed the control setup from Hwangbo et al. (2019) and the survey. The policy does not output torques directly. It outputs joint position targets at 50 Hz, and a PD controller in Python (tau = kp(q_target − q) − kd·qdot) turns them into torques at every 5 ms physics step, clipped to the motor's torque limit.

I trained two controllers that are identical in every way except one. The "fixed" controller always trains on the nominal physics. The "randomized" controller uses domain randomization: at every episode reset, ground friction is sampled between 0.5× and 1.5× nominal, the mass of every link between 0.8× and 1.2×, and motor strength between 0.7× and 1.1×. Both use the same PPO hyperparameters, the same network, observation normalization, and the same 1.5 million timesteps, and I trained each one with 3 different random seeds, so randomization is the only difference. These three parameters come straight from the survey, which lists randomizing friction, mass, and motor strength or gains as one of the main sim-to-real techniques. When I scale mass, I also scale each link's inertia, because Hwangbo et al. randomized the robot's inertial properties. I made "motor strength" scale both the PD output and the torque limit, so a weak motor really cannot produce the torque the policy expects. This is the kind of actuator mismatch that Hwangbo et al. describe, where policies trained on an idealized actuator model failed on the real ANYmal. Because the scale factors are always applied to the nominal values saved when the environment is created, the randomization never compounds from one episode to the next.

Since I do not have a real robot, I used changed simulators as a stand-in for "the real robot." After training, both controllers are tested on seven conditions with 50 episodes each: nominal physics, low friction (0.4×), high friction (1.6×), a heavy robot (1.3× mass), a light robot (0.7× mass), weak motors (0.6×), and a combined "pseudo-real" robot with 0.6× friction, 1.25× mass, and 0.75× motor strength. Most of these values are outside the training ranges on purpose, so the test measures generalization and not just whether the policy has seen those exact values before. Both controllers see exactly the same 50 starting states in each condition because the episode seeds are fixed, and the observation normalization statistics are frozen during testing. I also ran a friction sweep from 0.3× to 1.8×. An episode counts as a success if the robot's torso travels at least 5 m forward within the 10 second episode without falling. I chose 5 m because a good gait at the 1 m/s target covers about 10 m, so 5 m still requires real walking but leaves room for a slow start. It also means the fixed controller succeeds on nominal physics, so any drop later comes from the physics change and not from a weak baseline. The drop in success rate from nominal to pseudo-real is my stand-in for the sim-to-real gap.

## Results:

On nominal physics, both controllers succeeded in 100% of the test episodes on all 3 seeds, so any drop later comes from the physics change. As soon as the physics changed, the fixed controller fell apart. Averaged over the 3 seeds, it succeeded 0% of the time on the pseudo-real robot and on weak motors, 18% on the heavy robot, and 45% on low friction. That last number varied a lot between seeds (0%, 76%, and 60%). It only kept working on the "easier" shifts, high friction (100%) and the light robot (98%). The randomized controller succeeded 95% of the time on the pseudo-real robot, 100% on the heavy robot, and 95% on low friction. So the gap from nominal to pseudo-real shrank from 100 percentage points for the fixed controller to about 5 points for the randomized one. The hardest condition was weak motors (0.6×). Two randomized seeds passed it every time, but the third failed it completely, which gives a 67% average. This shows that randomization helps a lot but is not a guarantee. The main bar chart (success_rate_bar.png) shows orange "Fixed" bars that drop on low friction, heavy, weak motors, and pseudo-real, next to blue "Randomized" bars that mostly stay near 100%. The gap summary (gap_summary.png) puts the 100 point and 5 point gaps side by side. In the friction sweep (friction_sweep.png), the fixed controller is fine at 0.75× friction and above but drops to 66% at 0.6×, 56% at 0.45×, and 6% at 0.3×. The randomized controller stays at 100% down to 0.45× and still gets 74% at 0.3×. I also looked at why the fixed controller falls on low friction. Its first push slips, which breaks its bounding rhythm, and the robot then tips forward onto its front leg within about half a second.

The training curves (training_curves.png) show the cost of randomization that the readings mention. Each training run took about 9 minutes on a 4-core CPU. On average, the fixed controller first covered 5 m per episode after about 150,000 timesteps and reached 95% of its final reward after about 280,000. The randomized controller needed about 212,000 and 440,000 timesteps, so it learned more slowly, but both ended at about the same reward. I also tested some effects that I did not randomize, as a preview of a phase-2 project. With 40 ms of actuator delay, the randomized controller dropped to 9% success. A third controller that also randomized delay, sensor noise, and PD gains stayed at 100% (one seed). This shows that randomization only protects against what you randomize, which matches Hwangbo et al.'s point that unmodeled actuator effects break policies on real robots. This experiment has clear limitations. The robot is planar, so it cannot fall sideways and is much simpler than a real quadruped. The "real robot" is itself a simulator, so it does not include all the effects of real hardware. I only tested one robot design, and I used 3 seeds per controller (and only one for the phase-2 controller), so the error bars are wide in places such as weak motors.
