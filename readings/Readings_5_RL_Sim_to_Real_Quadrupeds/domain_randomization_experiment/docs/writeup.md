# Readings 5: Domain Randomization Experiment

## Program Explanation:

This program is a small sim-to-real experiment. It tests whether domain randomization makes a learned walking controller hold up when the physics it meets are different from the physics it trained on. The robot is my own MuJoCo model: a planar two-legged "bounding" robot that works like a 2D slice through a quadruped. It has a torso, a back leg and a front leg, and a hip and a knee on each leg. The process begins by training a walking policy with PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization) from Stable-Baselines3. The reward pays the robot for moving forward up to a target speed of 1.0 m/s, gives a small alive bonus each step, and subtracts small penalties for torque use and for tilting the body. The episode ends as soon as the robot falls. I chose PPO because the Gurram, Uttam & Ohol (2024) survey names it as the most common algorithm in RL quadruped locomotion. I also followed the control setup from Hwangbo et al. (2019) and the survey. The policy does not output torques directly. It outputs joint position targets at 50 Hz, and a PD controller in Python (tau = kp(q_target − q) − kd·qdot) turns them into torques at every 5 ms physics step, clipped to the motor's torque limit.

I trained two controllers that are identical in every way except one. The "fixed" controller always trains on the nominal physics. The "randomized" controller uses domain randomization: at every episode reset, ground friction is sampled between 0.5× and 1.5× nominal, the mass of every link between 0.8× and 1.2×, and motor strength between 0.7× and 1.1×. Both use the same PPO hyperparameters, the same network, observation normalization, and the same 1.5 million timesteps, so randomization is the only difference. These three parameters come straight from the survey, which lists randomizing friction, mass, and motor strength or gains as one of the main sim-to-real techniques. When I scale mass, I also scale each link's inertia, because Hwangbo et al. randomized the robot's inertial properties. I made "motor strength" scale both the PD output and the torque limit, so a weak motor really cannot produce the torque the policy expects. This is the kind of actuator mismatch that Hwangbo et al. describe, where policies trained on an idealized actuator model failed on the real ANYmal. Because the scale factors are always applied to the nominal values saved when the environment is created, the randomization never compounds from one episode to the next.

Since I do not have a real robot, I used changed simulators as a stand-in for "the real robot." After training, both controllers are tested on seven conditions with 50 episodes each: nominal physics, low friction (0.4×), high friction (1.6×), a heavy robot (1.3× mass), a light robot (0.7× mass), weak motors (0.6×), and a combined "pseudo-real" robot with 0.6× friction, 1.25× mass, and 0.75× motor strength. Most of these values are outside the training ranges on purpose, so the test measures generalization and not just whether the policy has seen those exact values before. Both controllers see exactly the same 50 starting states in each condition because the episode seeds are fixed, and the observation normalization statistics are frozen during testing. I also ran a friction sweep from 0.3× to 1.8×. An episode counts as a success if the robot's torso travels at least 5 m forward within the 10 second episode without falling. I chose 5 m because a good gait at the 1 m/s target covers about 10 m, so 5 m still requires real walking but leaves room for a slow start. It also means the fixed controller succeeds on nominal physics, so any drop later comes from the physics change and not from a weak baseline. The drop in success rate from nominal to pseudo-real is my stand-in for the sim-to-real gap.

## Results:

On nominal physics both controllers succeeded in 100% of the 50 test episodes, with mean distances of 12.8 m (fixed) and 13.3 m (randomized). As soon as the physics changed, the fixed controller fell apart. It succeeded 0% of the time on low friction, 2% on the heavy robot, 0% on weak motors, and 0% on the pseudo-real robot, falling in almost every episode and covering only 0.3 to 1.6 m on average. It only kept working on the "easier" shifts, high friction (100%) and the light robot (100%). The randomized controller succeeded 100% of the time in all seven conditions, including the ones outside its training range. It slowed down when the motors were weaker (8.6 m on weak motors and 10.2 m on the pseudo-real robot, compared to 13.3 m nominal), but it never fell. So the gap from nominal to pseudo-real shrank from 100 percentage points for the fixed controller to 0 points for the randomized one. The main bar chart (success_rate_bar.png) shows this as orange "Fixed" bars that drop to zero on low friction, heavy, weak motors, and pseudo-real, next to blue "Randomized" bars that stay at 100% everywhere. The gap summary plot (gap_summary.png) shows the same thing as two arrows from nominal to pseudo-real. The friction sweep (friction_sweep.png) shows that the fixed controller works for friction of 0.9× and above (97 to 100%), drops to 87% at 0.75×, and fails below that (3 to 7% at 0.45 to 0.6×, 0% at 0.3×). The randomized controller stayed at 100% from 0.3× all the way to 1.8×.

The training curves (training_curves.png) show the cost of randomization that the readings mention. Each training run took about 10 minutes on a 4-core CPU. The fixed controller first averaged 5 m per episode after about 143,000 timesteps and reached 95% of its final reward at about 250,000 timesteps. The randomized controller needed about 205,000 and 455,000 timesteps for the same milestones, so it learned roughly half as fast. Both ended at about the same reward (around 1,225 per episode). In other words, randomization slowed training down a little but gave a much more robust policy. This experiment has clear limitations. The robot is planar, so it cannot fall sideways and is much simpler than a real quadruped. The "real robot" is itself a simulator, so it does not include things a real robot has, like sensor noise, latency, or actuator dynamics such as the ones Hwangbo et al. modeled with their actuator network. I only tested one robot design. I trained only one seed per condition because of time, so the error bars are binomial standard errors over the 50 episodes rather than spread across seeds. The project supports more seeds with one setting, and a stronger version of this experiment would use at least three.
